PlaNeT SimTech 2025/26

Blautopf heat-extraction circuit

Removing heat from the karst inflow before the spring boils

Problem statement
The Blautopf is a karst spring in Blaubeuren near Ulm. A constant increase in the deep water temperature is expected to make the water boil in ten years. How can we permanently prevent the water in the Blautopf from reaching its boiling point? The measures should be as inexpensive and economical as possible. [1]
Team ID: TEST-0000
Date: 7 November 2026, English test edition
Competition: PlaNeT SimTech

Summary

We propose cooling the water underground before it flows into the Blautopf. Wells pump it to the surface, where heat exchangers remove the heat. The same quantity of water then returns to the underground river. No machinery is installed in the visible spring basin.

The required capacity follows directly from the stated temperature trend. In year ten, 11.77 gigawatts of heat must be removed at maximum discharge. A small cooling coil in the spring basin cannot do this. The smaller system with fixed heat input applies only if measurements instead confirm a fixed heat source.

Our system first supplies heat to buildings or industrial sites. Dry coolers reject the remaining heat to the air. Electric trim cooling starts only when these stages are insufficient. The 160 modules provide 12.8 gigawatts in total. If one module fails, 12.72 gigawatts remain available.

The cost scenario estimates construction at maximum discharge at €4.91 billion. Electricity demand in year ten is 0.03 terawatt-hours at low flow, 0.30 at mean flow, and 4.37 at maximum flow. Over twenty years, costs for mean to maximum flow range from €7.76 billion to €14.42 billion if temperature remains constant after year ten, and from €11.92 billion to €18.98 billion if it continues rising. Undiscounted life-cycle costs are infinite under unlimited growth. The present value of linearly growing payments converges at 3%.

Answer. Unlimited linear temperature growth has no finite permanent solution. The heat-extraction circuit is a temporary bridge. It can protect the spring permanently if temperature stabilizes after year ten. If temperature continues rising, the heat source must be controlled. We include a €250 million assessment path with an exploration well and grouting. If that proves infeasible, no finite permanent solution remains possible.

1. Introduction and approach

The Blautopf connects a large karst catchment with the River Blau. Rain infiltrates the Swabian Alb and sometimes reaches the spring within a few days. Mean discharge is 2.27 m³/s, with a range from 0.25 to 32.67 m³/s. Its natural temperature is about 9 °C. [2][3] These values make the task a problem of flowing water. The small volume in the visible spring basin is only a short-term buffer.

We address four questions. How much heat does the forecast produce? At which discharge is the largest intervention required? Where can the heat go without harming the Blautopf and Blau? What limits a permanent solution?

Method

  1. We apply the linear temperature rise from 9 to 98.3 °C over ten years directly to the inflow.
  2. We derive the required cooling capacity from the energy balance and examine minimum, mean, and maximum discharge.
  3. We compare dilution with cold water, cooling in the spring basin, and our heat-extraction circuit.
  4. We design a controllable cascade, calculate a summer case and an annual scenario, and examine costs, failures, and environmental risks.

Our contribution

Our design combines power-based flood dilution, a hydraulically closed heat-extraction circuit, and a demand-controlled cooling cascade. The control variable is the measured heat flow rather than temperature in the spring basin alone. The system can therefore respond to changes in inflow before the spring warms noticeably.

Model assumptions

A1. Design case B follows the stated linear temperature rise at the inflow. Heat input therefore rises in proportion to discharge, and high flow is the most expensive case. Model A is only an alternative. A localized heat source with fixed power would be diluted by additional water. We do not assume that model.

A2. The heat source distributes its power across the inflow under consideration. Cold water from other karst areas mixes with it. We neglect heat losses to rock and air in the natural case, which is conservative. Once boiling begins, the natural model no longer provides a complete water-steam balance, so we do not evaluate it further.

A3. The heat-extraction circuit returns the entire pumped mass flow. The underground discharge remains unchanged in the balance. At high flow, an uncooled share may bypass the system because dilution lowers the temperature.

A4. After year ten, heat input either remains constant or continues growing linearly. In the first case, the fixed system suffices with maintenance. In the second, only an adaptive path is possible. Unlimited growth requires unlimited capacity and produces infinite undiscounted costs. No fixed finite expansion can suffice.

A5. Weather, prices, and investments are scenario values rather than quotations. Heat sales are not safety-critical. The circuit is designed for 14 bar in hot years. Water remains liquid at this pressure and 187.6 °C in year 20. The total head of 140 m includes the assumed drawdown of 100 m plus 40 m for static elevation differences, pipe losses, pressure maintenance, and injection. Drawdown is therefore not counted twice.

Cause still unknown. Volcanic activity is a hypothesis, not a measurement. Our system works independently of the cause. Site planning nevertheless requires temperature profiles, water chemistry, gases, seismic data, and tracer tests. Only these data can show where the heat enters each inflow and in what quantity.

3. Mathematical model

Design based on the temperature trend

Temperature rises from 9 to 98.3 °C in ten years. With 8.93 K/year and P = ρcQΔT, we obtain:

T(t) = 9 °C + 8.93 K/year · t and PK(t,Q) = ρcQ·max[0, T(t)−12 °C]

The stated trend is therefore the design case. We stop examining the natural state after boiling begins because the system acts before that point.

Design of the heat-extraction circuit

Figure 1: Extraction and injection are located in the inflow, not in the visible spring basin. [10]

Tracer tests and geophysics determine well locations outside the spring area. Directional drilling protects caves, while packers separate cold injected water from warm intake water.

In design case B, the temperature is 98.3 °C throughout. For a mixed temperature of 12 °C, q·9.5 °C + qB·98.3 °C = Q·12 °C. This gives q = 31.750 m³/s and qB = 0.920 m³/s. The heat balance gives ρcq(98.3−9.5) = 11.77 GW. The normal target of 9.5 °C requires pumping the entire discharge.

After one failure, 23 pairs at 1.55 m³/s each provide 34.10 m³/s. The assumed yield of 15.5 L/s per metre produces 100 m of drawdown, which is included in the total head of 140 m. The process flow of 31.75 m³/s requires 58.02 MW at 75% efficiency. Using 67 low-yield pairs at 0.5 m³/s each adds €440 million. Pilot wells must test these assumptions. Alternative A uses 14 pairs and 18.35 m³/s at 12 °C. At 9.5 °C and maximum discharge under alternative A costs, it uses 23 pairs and 32.67 m³/s, with additional costs of €90 million.

5. Cooling cascade and control

Three stages instead of one large chiller

Stage 1 uses heat. Heat exchangers first supply a heating network or industrial site. Blaubeuren is already investigating an expansion of its heating networks. [9] The safety calculation assumes no heat sales.

Stage 2 uses dry cooling. Closed water-to-air coolers reach air temperature plus 10 K in the base scenario. There is no evaporation and no discharge into the Blau. Their fans consume 1% of total heat rejection, including condenser heat from the refrigeration cycle.

Stage 3 provides trim cooling. The refrigeration cycle covers the remaining load. Evaporation at 7 °C and condensation at 45 °C give a Carnot COP of 7.37. Our efficiency scenario of 0.55 gives COP = 4.05. [5] Fully mechanical cooling to 9.5 °C requires 3.20 GW in year 10 and about 6.35 GW in year 20, including auxiliary power. Two 7.5 GW grid connections and a 7.0 GW backup power system cover trim cooling, fans, and pumps throughout the growth path.

Control rules instead of price optimization

Heat meters measure ρcq(Tin−Tout) on each line. Their sum controls the cascade. The system first supplies confirmed heat customers, then the dry coolers. If this cannot maintain 9.5 °C, trim cooling follows. At 11 °C, heat sales and price logic are disabled. At 12 °C, all available modules operate. If a sensor fails, the higher reading from two independent probes is used.

Why 160 modules

The 160 modules provide 12.8 GW. One failure leaves 12.72 GW, a reserve of 5.1% above normal demand of 12.11 GW. This reserve covers fouling and maintenance. With 159 modules, one failure would leave only 4.4% reserve. In year 20, 321 modules are installed. One failure then leaves 25.6 GW, a reserve of about 5.4%.

8. Critical discussion

What the model establishes

The stated temperature trend requires 11.77 GW at maximum discharge. This order of magnitude depends little on equipment details. The heat-extraction circuit closes the balance without consuming water or rejecting waste heat to the Blau. Its costs make it especially important to investigate the heat source and possible interventions there.

Questions the current calculation cannot answer

  • Thermal capture. We do not know the share or pathways of heated deep water. If several inflows mix only in the spring basin, one well pair will not suffice. Tracers and temperature profiles must determine capture effectiveness.
  • Forecast form. A local deep measurement need not apply to the entire inflow. The report conservatively applies it to that inflow. Alternative A must remain strictly separate. The cause remains unknown.
  • Karst hydraulics. Extraction and injection can alter flow paths. A coupled groundwater and heat-transport model must calculate short circuits, pressure, and interaction with other inflows.
  • Ecology. The 12 °C limit is a technical ceiling, not permission for 3 K of warming. The normal target of 9.5 °C must be checked against species, seasonal variation, and oxygen.
  • Costs. The cost range is a sensitivity analysis, not a statistical confidence interval. Karst drilling in particular could change the price substantially.

Operating and material risks

Calcareous water can form deposits when pressure drops and gas escapes. We keep the groundwater circuit pressurized and use double-wall, cleanable heat exchangers. Differential pressure and heat transfer reveal fouling early. [7] The closed secondary circuit prevents contamination. A leak between the walls triggers shutdown.

Backup power and redundancy reduce failure probabilities but do not eliminate them. Natural hazards and common-cause failures require a separate risk analysis. Without these data, claiming that boiling will "never again" occur would be too strong.

Decisions under uncertainty

We do not recommend immediate full-scale construction. The first steps are a measurement network and a three-module pilot plant. Its energy balance determines whether the heat-extraction circuit reaches the hot inflow. Expansion follows only after that test. The historical minimum discharge need not persist from the start of the project. If such a drought threatens before commissioning, rented mobile refrigeration modules with 120 MW and their own power supply provide interim cooling. We estimate one year at €20 million for rental plus about €30 million for electricity. This is a scenario, not a guaranteed schedule. Permitting, delivery, and construction remain critical paths.

Conclusion

Boiling can be prevented by removing excess heat from the underground inflow. Our heat-extraction circuit pumps heated water, cools it outside the spring basin, and injects the same quantity back. The stated temperature trend requires 11.77 GW at maximum discharge in year ten. A cascade of heat recovery, dry cooling, and mechanical trim cooling maintains at most 12 °C. Normal operation remains close to the natural temperature.

Costs depend on the forecast interpretation. Only in alternative A does high water provide relief through dilution and permit the fixed-heat-input system. In design case B, high discharge determines the 160 modules, wells, and costs. The measurement campaign must resolve this distinction.

The solution is complex, but its physics are closed and it can be tested in stages. It consumes no cooling water and rejects no heat to the Blau. The largest risks are the heat source, karst hydraulics, and drilling costs. With more development time, we would add hourly extremes, a coupled groundwater and heat-transport model, and firm cost quotations. The measurement campaign and pilot plant are therefore integral to this proposal.

Permanence requires adaptation. The heat-extraction circuit starts as a bridge. With maintenance and reserve, it suffices permanently if temperature stabilizes after year ten. If temperature continues rising, the source must be controlled. Our assessment path costs €250 million. If such control is infeasible, no finite permanent solution exists.

Checking the alternatives

For mixing, TM = (QhTh + QkTk)/(Qh + Qk). Solving for the cold-water share gives Qk/Qh = (Th−TM)/(TM−Tk). With 98.3 °C, 12 °C, and 9 °C, this ratio is 28.77. At the design discharge of 32.67 m³/s, Qk = 28.77 × 32.67 m³/s = 939.8 m³/s. This is no longer local dilution but a new river.

For surface cooling, P = UAΔT. With P = 11.767 GW, U = 500 W/(m²K), and ΔT = 30 K, A = 784,467 m². The value of U is deliberately generous to this alternative and is not a measured coefficient for a free water surface. Even then, the required area is more than two orders of magnitude too large. A heat pump in the spring basin would also have to reject its condenser heat elsewhere.

Present value

For each payment Kt in year t, PV = Σ Kt/(1+i)t. For Kt = a + bt and t = 1, 2, ..., PV = a/i + b(1+i)/i² converges at i = 0.03, including linear growth. The initial payment comprises €110 million in fixed costs and three modules. Each additional module costs €30 million in its construction year. Operation and maintenance are calculated annually. Replacement investment in year 15 is €200 million × module count in year 15/13, or €3,523.08 million on the growth path. The first year includes €50 million for emergency cooling. This produces €14,424.20 million at constant temperature and €18,984.91 million on the growth path. The €250 million source-control assessment in year 10, discounted at 3%, raises the growth present value to €19,170.92 million.
